01

What is actually being measured

Nobody moves south because Naples got warm. They move because Cleveland got cold. That distinction sets up the whole analysis: the quantity that should drive migration is not a temperature but a gap — how much warmer Naples is than the place someone keeps their other house.

So the project tracks two ends of a journey. Daily temperatures for Naples, and daily temperatures for the northern states that actually send people here, each weighted by how many they send. The difference between them is the invitation. When it is wide, the trip is worth making; when it closes, people go home.

That framing produced a tidy first result, and then the tidy first result fell apart. What follows is the order it happened in, because the order is the argument.

02

The finding that did not survive

Defining a “migration window” as the period when the gap exceeds 25 °F gave a clean answer across 26 seasons: the window was opening 4.7 days later per decade, p = 0.037. The autumn edge had drifted roughly twelve days since 2000 while the spring edge had not moved. A real asymmetry, apparently.

The trouble is that 25 °F was chosen by hand. So the analysis was re-run at every threshold from 12 °F to 36 °F, under two different weighting schemes, and the result was put on a chart rather than in a sentence.

Two hits out of twenty-five is what pure chance delivers. The significant results clustered in a narrow band around 21–25 °F and appeared nowhere else — and that band happens to contain the number originally picked. The effect was an artefact of where the line was drawn.

Correction

The temperature data does not show the migration window shifting. Weighting states by real migration figures did not rescue it. The headline result of the first three scripts was discarded, and the dashboard now leads with the forecast instead.

03

Measuring people instead of thermometers

Everything above measures weather and calls it migration. The fix was FDOT’s traffic database — a 1.55 GB Access file containing hour-by-hour counts from machines embedded in the road. For 2024 it gives every day of the year at fourteen stations across Collier and Lee.

Put real traffic against the temperature gap and the two curves do move together, but not in step. Traffic turns up about 25 days after the thermometer says it should, and leaves about 25 days after the weather stops justifying it. Temperature explains under half the daily variation (r = 0.66).

A lag at both ends is the informative part. If people were responding to weather they would arrive when it started paying and leave when it stopped. Arriving late and leaving late is what a calendar looks like: after Thanksgiving, after Easter. Weather sets the backdrop; the diary picks the date.

Near miss

The first run reported the quietest stretch of 2024 as 29 September — a plausible late-summer lull. It was Hurricane Helene. Three storms hit that year, and the signature is unmistakable: traffic at 1.53× normal on 8 October as people evacuated, then 0.30× on Milton’s landfall. Removing the storm windows flipped the sign of the autumn result, from traffic appearing to lead the thermometer by 11 days to lagging it by 25.

04

The models, and why each one

Harmonic regression — the seasonal shape

A season is a wave, so waves are fitted to it: sine and cosine pairs at one, two, three and four cycles per year, plus day-of-week terms so quiet Sundays are not mistaken for a seasonal dip. The model is fitted to the logarithm of the traffic index, which makes effects multiplicative — a coefficient means “+12%”, not “+12 cars”. R² = 0.76.

Each station is first converted to an index against its own average day. That is what lets a rural Everglades road on 3,660 vehicles a day be averaged with 12-0184 on 125,228.

Moving-block bootstrap — the intervals

An ordinary bootstrap assumes every day is independent, which is nonsense for traffic: a busy Tuesday implies a busy Wednesday. Resampling contiguous 14-day blocks of the residuals preserves that stickiness, so the confidence intervals are not fantasy. 600 replicates.

Linear trend fits — the year-over-year questions

Ordinary least squares, used only where the question is genuinely “is this drifting?”, and always reported with the sweep or the exclusion test that shows whether the answer holds up.

Engineering note

The bootstrap originally refitted the model from scratch 200 times when only the response vector changed. Factoring the design matrix once with a QR decomposition turned each replicate into a back-substitution: 4.61 s to 0.16 s. Verifying the fast path against the slow one caught a real bug — wday() returns an ordered factor, which R codes with polynomial contrasts, so a rebuilt design matrix silently paired coefficients with the wrong basis. The curve still looked plausible, and was wrong by 0.5%.

05

Where they come from

The origin states began as a guess: Chicago, Detroit, Cleveland, New York, Boston, Toronto. Two better instruments replaced it.

IRS county-to-county migration records every household that changed its tax address into Collier County. That corrected the guess: New Jersey is a top-four source and had been left out entirely, while Ohio is minor and its migration-weighted centre sits in mid-Ohio, nowhere near Cleveland.

But IRS data can only see people who stopped being northerners, and a classic snowbird deliberately does not. So the second instrument is Florida property tax rolls: a residential parcel whose owner gets mail out of state and claims no homestead exemption is a second home, by definition rather than by proxy. There are 62,572 of them — 28% of every home in Collier County.

The disagreement is not noise, it is a pattern. Midwesterners keep their northern domicile; high-tax north-easterners make the move permanent. The two datasets are measuring two different behaviours, and the gap between them is itself the finding.

What IRS data cannot see

Ontario owns 3,959 Naples homes — enough to rank seventh if it were a US state. Canadians file no US return, so migration records put it at zero. The original guess of “Toronto” was right; the correction that removed it was wrong.

Hurricanes and timing

Tested across 36 clean seasons using the autumn El Niño index as an instrument — it is the main driver of Atlantic activity, it is exogenous, and it runs back to 1950. No relationship (r = −0.13, p = 0.45), and the sign runs backwards from the intuition. Two reasons: El Niño also warms northern winters, so the pathways partly cancel; and hurricane risk is priced in before the season, so the absence of a storm cannot pull anyone earlier. Nobody knows October was quiet until it is over.

Becoming permanent

Across twelve years of IRS records the rise looks solid — +360 people a year, p = 0.013. Remove 2020–22 and it collapses to +140 a year, p = 0.19. 2021 alone brought 11,566 northern arrivals against a pre-COVID norm near 7,000. That is the remote-work exodus, not a gradual conversion.

Data trap

IRS FIPS codes are zero-padded in most editions (“021”) but not in the 2020–21 and 2021–22 files (“21”). Matching on the string silently dropped two years — no error, no warning. Correcting it changed the headline from “no clear trend” to significant. Two missing points.

Winter is arriving slightly late up north, which leans the dates marginally later rather than earlier. Meanwhile a strong El Niño is holding the hurricane season quiet.

That second point matters in a specific and limited way. The model was fitted to 2024 — a year with three storms in it. A quiet season should therefore make this September’s trough shallower than the model suggests. It should not move it: the test in section 06 found no relationship between storm activity and arrival timing. The date should hold; the depth should not.

08

What this forecast cannot do

The binding constraint is not the method, it is the data. The seasonal shape is fitted to one year of daily counts, because FDOT overwrite the detailed tables with each annual edition rather than accumulating them. The confidence intervals therefore say how precisely we know 2024 — not how much the season moves between years, which one year cannot tell you. Treat them as a floor.

Weather forecasting cannot fill the gap, and this was tested rather than assumed. NOAA’s 50-member seasonal ensemble was compared against 26 years of plain climatology for the same dates. Beyond 60 days the ensemble spread is 1.12× climatology — it is wider than simply knowing what month it is. For a March peak it carries no information at all. Deterministic models hit a hard skill horizon around 10–15 days; that is chaos, not compute.

Two other limits worth stating plainly. Traffic counts vehicles, not people — a resident who drives twice a day counts twice. And the roads disagree with each other: peak timing spans two months across stations, and amplitude varies twofold, from 17% at one station to 34% in the rural Everglades. If you care about a specific road, the county average will mislead you.

The single highest-value improvement would be obtaining prior-year FTI editions from FDOT’s Transportation Data & Analytics office. A second year of daily counts would, for the first time, allow the year-to-year variation in the season to be measured instead of assumed.

Built in R. Analysis in fifteen scripts; forecast from a harmonic regression with moving-block bootstrap intervals; a scheduled watcher re-checks every source daily and raises an alert if a headline date moves, naming the source that caused it.
